I'm scared of distractions. There are many distractions in SF: hackathons, coffee chats, WhatsApp intros. Furthermore, you're expected to be in the know, which also means distractions. By default, you'll become a sleazy dopamine sucker, losing your ability to do what's hard but worthwhile. Unfortunately, this is the attractive equilibrium. Aaa!

As a student in Zürich, I didn't face this problem: there was my dorm room, MathBib, Polymensa and ASVZ. My day was a closed loop, starting and ending at Studenthaus Dahno[^price]. However, as a professional, you're expected to interface with the outside world. You obviously can't cut out corporate events and news -- these are opportunities for growth. But how can you make such opportunities less of a distraction? Here's my attempt at classifying sources of distraction, and fixing good boundary conditions, if you will.

[^price]: The ridiculous prices for concerts in Zürich naturally helped with locking in.

### Meetings

You need to take meetings with people outside your org, ideally in person. Here are ideas on avoiding scheduling overhead and making meetings more pleasant.

- Walk and talk; two birds in one stone. Apparently lots of famous entrepreneurs liked doing this -- Steve Jobs, Mark Zuckerberg, Jack Dorsey. I've also come to learn it's a big thing at [Standard Intelligence](https://si.inc).
- Pass on meeting links. Standard, but I had to mention this. My wish for 2027 is that sending meeting links will become socially acceptable in non-EA settings.
- Use AI admin tools like Instinct, Dots, Muse or Energy for scheduling ping pong. While I'm generally skeptical of AI's ability to improve our everyday lives, I can confidently say that Instinct has saved me a ton of time. I'll send Instinct an audio message gesturing at what I want the email to say, and -- boom -- I get an email draft written in my style.
- Keep a meeting-free block each day. I try taking meetings in the late afternoon or evening, since I find meetings pretty energising anyway[^lu]. Mornings are earmarked for demanding work.
- Say no. Basic, but I haven't had to learn this skill up until now. AI admin tools can help you assess when a meeting's worth it.
- Do a thorough meeting prep, but do it efficiently. I normally try finding a podcast with whoever I'm meeting. Not only do you get insider takes you can bring up later -- you also get to learn how they speak, which, if you're prone to stuttering or umming, can be helpful. If I cannot find a podcast episode, I'll make Gemini Notebook generate a podcast about some piece of public output. My meeting prep philosophy, in short, is to podcastify as much as possible. I have a lot of dead time -- [podcast surface](https://isabeldahlgren.github.io/analytic-podcast-listening/) -- and would rather spend desktop time doing something productive.

[^lu]: Thankfully, CET is on my side.

### News

We need news. An annoying fact of life: if you want to stay up to date with AI, you need Twitter. I originally got on Twitter in June, to repost [Thomas Bloom's kind tweet about Diderot](https://x.com/thomasfbloom/status/2069330172440928577). Once I figured out how to curate my feed (follow at most 150 carefully selected people), I came to like it. Scrolling Twitter is much like reading La Rochefoucauld's maxims.

Twitter has become the place to announce major maths breakthroughs, new papers and to yap. It was fascinating following mathematical history unfold in real time in Twitter threads about [the Jacobian conjecture](https://x.com/__alpoge__/status/2079028340955197566?lang=en) and [Navier-Stokes](https://x.com/__alpoge__/status/2097548261666033993). Then there's also just a lot of [thoughtful commentary](https://x.com/sebkrier/status/2107478899529527563) and [wonderfully brain-dead fun](https://x.com/rovvmut_/status/2105154457113972959).

However, using Twitter deliberately is very different from doomscrolling (what you'll do unless you have bespoke Twitter rules). You don't need to know everything, and you also need op-eds every now and then. So, here's my current news strategy:

- Read the [Paradigm3 newsletter](https://p3humansonai.substack.com). It's both nuanced and well-written[^anti], and has broad coverage. In my view, it's the best digest out there; I don't think [*Don't Worry About the Vase*](https://thezvi.substack.com/?utm_campaign=profile_chips), the more well-established alternative, is of nearly as high quality. Since the newsletter is somewhat technical, I read it on my laptop, as opposed to listening to the audio version, typically over meals.
- Limit Twitter to three times a day. Twitter is great for meals. Meals are great for chill scrolling.
- Read some normal news too. It's helpful knowing how normal people think, after all. I'm a long-time fan of [*Under strecket*](https://www.svd.se/kultur/under-strecket), daily essays published in one of Sweden's leading newspapers. They recently published a series of essays on intelligence, and I've found it refreshing reading real philosophy profs. For example, the [opening essay](https://www.svd.se/a/4EdaO9/den-missforstadda-intelligensen) poignantly notes that the word 'intelligence' usually isn't used to denote problem-solving skills, but instead a kind of 'rapidity of the soul'. Now that expression isn't tech bros philolarping -- wonderful!
- You don't need to be omniscient; you just need to know the right information. For me, the right information is a combination of risk assessment reports, technical papers and econ. I try reading breaking papers the same day they come out, and I believe reading many papers pays off[^dan]. To quickly absorb a paper, I'll first have a look at any accompanying blog post or Twitter thread; if it seems interesting, I read the full paper. Overall, though, I place a lot of trust in the newsletter editors -- they're my $L^1$ penalty. And if you can't trust your newsletter editors, it's a bad newsletter.

[^dan]: [Daniel Paleka](https://danielpaleka.com) originally offered me this advice.

### Clutter

Random thoughts can also be a distraction. In fact, this is probably the biggest source of distraction for me. Scheduling is boring, most news is boring, but there are so many lovely thoughts out there -- how can I resist? Should I resist? Finally, I'll discuss my current stack for creating a comfy headspace.

- Keep your phone muted in 4-hour blocks, but generally try answering ASAP. I've deliberated how to handle incoming messages -- I used to try responding to messages at a designated time each day -- and I think this strategy strikes a balance between responding quickly and batching. If responding will take more than 10 minutes, I might create a reminder to answer, e.g. using the [Reminders plugin for Raycast](https://www.raycast.com/raycast/apple-reminders), or asking Instinct to remind me about it at a later date.
- Get the morning right, and you'll get the rest of the day right. At least I function this way. If I wake up at 7am, read *Under strecket* over breakfast and head off to work at 8am, I feel like Zeno for the rest of the day.
- Notice FOMO, spend 30 seconds asking whether it's justified, conclude it isn't, get back to work. As with many undesirable feelings, registering them helps. This is easier said than done, though. Self-awareness is hard!
- Use AI assistants for task management, i.e. what to do and when to do it. Even if Instinct is imperfect, it does a much better job than my gut feeling (blubby blub, red dot on WhatsApp, blub, what was it again).

### It's about the fun, stupid

You can't get rid of distractions, but you can try owning them, in the ways described above.

I'm still scared about distractions. I take pleasure in maintaining a singular focus, or, in [founderspeak](https://isabeldahlgren.github.io/founderspeak/), locking in. For me, handling distractions isn't so much about productivity gains, but about enjoyment.
