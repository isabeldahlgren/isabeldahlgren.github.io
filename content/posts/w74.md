Prompt engineering, the process of designing good prompts for generative AI models, is dead, [say some](https://www.reddit.com/r/PromptEngineering/comments/1rci46t/prompt_engineering_is_dead_in_2026/). Long gone are the days when you needed to prefix your prompt with 'Act as an expert...',  provide few-shot examples or spell out each step.

Lately, I've spent more time writing prompts. Less time in the terminal with Claude Code, more time with Claude desktop. While I used to vibe code systems for automating drudge work, today it's sometimes more efficient prompting the chatbot directly, and having it orchestrate the workflow itself. Chances are it'll deliver a folder with supplementary code anyway -- the code I would've vibe coded, only better.

For example, I recently built a pipeline for generating interesting mathematical research questions and answering them. I'd supply my favourite papers within my area, much as a seed, and make agents formulate research questions, coming up with solutions and typeset the findings. At each step, I'd make other agents adversarially screen the output, asking the agent to redo a step if it wasn't up to standards -- I'd have rubrics for research taste and correctness in markdown documents. With this scaffolding, an everyday model like Sonnet could produce decent results. However, the whole system was too token-intensive, and running the pipeline end to end took a few hours[^opus].

[^opus]: Due to cost constraints, I couldn't run the system with Opus 4.8.

Instead, I had an AI produce a [prompt](https://github.com/isabeldahlgren/papers/blob/main/equiwidth-identifiability/PROMPT.md) to make GPT 5.6 Sol run the full pipeline at inference time, articulating questions, solving problems and presenting solutions, with quality checks at every step. Bitter lesson: it works. Giving a chatbot a carefully written prompt proved more time- and cost-effective.

It shouldn't come as a surprise -- some AI labs are quite explicit about their goal to automate intellectual work. The first paragraph in OpenAI's mission statement reads:

> OpenAI’s mission is to ensure that artificial general intelligence (AGI)—by which we mean highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work—benefits all of humanity. We will attempt to directly build safe and beneficial AGI, but will also consider our mission fulfilled if our work aids others to achieve this outcome.

Until now, I hadn't fully internalised this was their goal. Read literally, automating intellectual work means reducing work to writing one prompt and having the model one-shot the task in the desktop app.

This has made me question the utility (and viability) of many AI scaffolding products. There are plenty of specialised AI tools for generating and reviewing written work, whether legal reports, research articles or op-eds. Bitter question[^stress]: if I generated a prompt asking a frontier model in thinking mode to execute exactly the same steps as my system, would I achieve comparable results? Unless the system draws on closed data sources, I think the answer is yes -- moreover, it's likely cheaper and faster. There is a sense in which many AI startups are just million-dollar prompt factories[^pro].

[^pro]: Think about it: if AI companies succeed in building general-purpose tools which can automate all economically valuable work, that leaves fewer niches for entrepreneurs.

[^stress]: Entrepreneurs may want to stress test their ideas with this question.

The frontier models accessible through desktop apps are already very good, and they integrate seamlessly with many services. It's safe to assume both model capabilities and integrations with other tools will become better. So, if you can craft the right prompt, then you can run the equivalent of a small startup, all by yourself.

I'd expect most companies to become AI companies -- companies which rely heavily on AI -- well within 10-20 years. Perhaps economists should start debating whether prompts are intellectual property?
