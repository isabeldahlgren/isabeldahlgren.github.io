Many tech workers are struck with a sense of wonder while visiting SF for the first time. The concentration of talent and capital makes SF an entrepreneur's Mecca. SF is also culturally siloed from the rest of the world: people feel the AGI, want to become cyborgs and wish to reverse aging.

I felt a sense of wonder both the first and second time. But I also felt angry -- how come no one told me about this strange place before? Jornos, historians, politicians, hello? There's some truth to the statement that everything happens in the US, whether you like it or not; ignorance isn't patriotism.

Here's what I want to tell everyone on earth.

### Pacifists and generals

In the magisterial [*From Counterculture to Cyberculture*](https://fredturner.stanford.edu/books/counterculture-cyberculture-stewart-brand-whole-earth-network-and-rise-digital-utopianism) (2005), Fred Turner argues that Silicon Valley culture is largely the product of the hippie movement and the military-industrial complex of WWII and the Cold War.

The hippies told us to build tools rather than products -- people can build what they need -- and that information gains value by circulating.

For example, the opening to the first edition of the influential *Whole Earth Catalog* emphasises the liberating function of technology. Since it reads much like an AI company mission statement, I'll quote it in full:

> We are as gods and might as well get good at it. So far, remotely done power and glory—as via government, big business, formal education, church—has succeeded to the point where gross defects obscure actual gains. In response to this dilemma and to these gains a realm of intimate, personal power is developing—power of the individual to conduct his own education, find his own inspiration, shape his own environment, and share his adventure with whoever is interested. Tools that aid this process are sought and promoted by the WHOLE EARTH CATALOG.

Or take Richard Brautigan's [*All Watched Over by Machines of Loving Grace*](https://www.readingdesign.org/all-watched-over-by-machines). I particularly like the first stanza:

> I like to think (and  
the sooner the better!)  
of a cybernetic meadow  
where mammals and computers  
live together in mutually  
programming harmony  
like pure water  
touching clear sky.

This sentiment is echoed by technooptimists today -- remember the title of [Dario's essay](https://darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace)?

The officers told us to collaborate interdisciplinarily. During the Second World War, the MIT Radiation Laboratory, alias the Rad Lab, was set up to improve radar for shooting down enemy aircraft. It brought together mathematicians, physicists, engineers and military planners. After the war, this morphed into the [MIT Research Laboratory of Electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Laboratory_of_Electronics), which became MIT's first interdepartmental research center. The more well-known MIT Media Lab and Santa Fe Institute, founded in 1984 and 1985, respectively, were modeled on the Research Laboratory of Electronics, and also emphasised interdisciplinary collaboration.

Now look at the defining products of the 21st century: the iPhone, Facebook, Tesla Model S... No engineer, physicist or designer could have created such a product alone -- they're the works of multidisciplinary teams[^buck].

[^buck]: Buckminster Fuller, who saw the need for engineer-designers ('an emerging synthesis of artist, inventor, mechanic, objective economist and evolutionary strategist') was right. See the concept of the [*comprehensive designer*](https://fredturner.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj27111/files/media/file/turner-buckminster-fuller-technocrat-for-the-cc.pdf).

### The drop-out mythos

Europeans will also be surprised by the cult of the drop-out. Bill Gates, Steve Jobs, Mark Zuckerberg all left college to found legendary companies. Dropping out makes for a better success story (which is what you're optimising for, no?) -- it's part of the Bay Area aesthetics.

The drop-out cult is hardly new, and it can be traced from the counterculture of the 1960s. The classic mantra 'turn on, tune in, drop out' was popularised in 1967 by Timothy Leary, a famous psychologist and advocate of psychedelics. These days, the priest of the drop-out cult is Peter Thiel, whose [fellowship](https://thielfellowship.org) backs drop-outs financially.

However, dropping out isn't a truly revolutionary act. Here's Adam Daub in *What Tech Calls Thinking*:

> Dropping out is still understood as a rejection of a certain elite. But it is an anti-elitism whose very point is to usher you as quickly as possible into another elite—the elite of those who are sufficiently tuned in, the elite of those who get it, the ones who see through the world that the squares are happy to inhabit.

This passage also broaches another interesting point: the drop-outs are disenchanted, otherworldly. Hence the *cult* of the drop-out.

Given that I know only a handful of European drop-outs, I didn't expect meeting that many drop-outs while in SF. But I met a good number of them, and they were among the most cracked founders I got to know. Today it's easier than ever just doing stuff, in large part due to AI, and a college education is 4 years less of tinkering.

### You are what you do

'What do you build?' Often strangers will ask about your company before your name.

SF culture is characterised by a fetishism for work. There are plenty of [tweets about hustle culture](https://x.com/stanleefounder/status/2085332994894557339), funny because they're partially true. In fact, Daub describes startups as 'aesthetic creations and as methods of self-realization'.

Interestingly, this ethos also runs through some Disney movies. If you agree with Daub's analysis of the movie *Ratatouille*, it should be the tech founder's favourite movie:

> In reality, the film is saying that if you’re a superspecial rat with God-given talent, you ought to be treated like the genius you are, no matter who you are. In the end, Ego probably summarizes the film’s central premise better: “Not everyone can become a great artist. But a great artist can come from anywhere.”

Your hobbies, family and friends are irrelevant to who you are. Ditto for your body: you're really just a brain on a stick, living in cyberspace.

### The need for speed

Bay Area culture is also defined by accelerationism[^fou], the idea that we ought to lean into change.

This partly explains the race dynamics among AI companies. China doesn't directly compete with OpenAI or Anthropic -- they're open-sourcing their models, and don't engage in benchmaxxing in the same way as American labs. Nick Land, the philosopher who popularised the ideology of accelerationism, wrote in his 2017 essay *A Quick-and-Dirty Introduction to Accelerationism* that 'thinking takes time, and accelerationism suggests we’re running out of time to think that through, if we haven’t already'. This mindset explains why OpenAI and Anthropic are pushing ahead.

In this [interview with Jasmin Sun](https://jasmi.news/p/from-counterculture-to-cyberculture), Turner highlights another eerie aspect of accelerationism, what he calls techno-facism. Futurism, in the sense of the Italian arts movement, was all about accelerationism, and, as we all know, the obsession with trains led to war and Mussolini. What will the obsession with AI lead to?

### Where we're heading

The technologists who come to SF describe the place as unreal. It's a fitting description: the founders want to transcend reality. They really do. Sometimes they succeed, tugging the world along with them. Time flows westward.
