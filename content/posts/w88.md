An inconvenient fact about theoretical deep learning: neural networks are messy functions. You're repeatedly composing nonlinear functions, usually well over a thousand times. This leads to what I'll dub the *modeling problem*:

> Find a model architecture that's analytically tractable, but also flexible.

Some candidate architectures include:
- The toy model from *Toy Models of Superposition*, $f(x) = \sigma(W^TWx+b)$ (with $\sigma = \operatorname{ReLU}$)
- Linear networks $f(x) = W_L \cdots W_1 x$

Another line of work focuses on networks with polynomial activation functions, *polynomial networks*. Pick your favourite architecture, and replace all activation functions with polynomials. That gives you so-called *algebraic machine learning models*, polynomial in both input variables and parameters.

The restriction to polynomial activations is relatively mild: Stone–Weierstraß tells us polynomials are dense in $C(X)$; therefore, you could expect polynomial networks to approximate the behaviour of those in the wild. Polynomial networks are, as indicated by the name, a natural generalisation of linear networks.

Once you've fixed a polynomial network, you obtain a *parametrisation* $\mathbf{\theta} \mapsto f_\theta$; this is the map which takes weights as input and spits out a neural network function[^sym].

[^sym]: In symbols: if $\mathcal{W}$ is the weight space and $\mathcal{F}$ a space of polynomials, the parametrisation is a map $\mathcal{W} \to \mathcal{F}$.

You can ask lots of questions regarding the parametrisation. For example:
- What are the fibres, i.e. the sets $\{\theta : f_\theta = f\}$ for some $f$?
- What are the critical points, if you differentiate w.r.t. each weight in $\mathbf{\theta}$?

We (Giovanni, Kathlén and I) just put out a paper studying these questions for polynomial residual networks. That is, we study networks recursively defined for $l \ge 1$ by $$f_l = (W_l f_{l-1} + b_l) + f_{l-1}, \quad f_0 = x.$$

The lil' $f_{l-1}$ term is called the *skip connection*. It turns out it makes a huge difference to the training dynamics: before people started adding skip connections to their networks, you could only train networks at most 30 layers deep; with skip connections, you could suddenly do thousands of layers. You could do deep learning!

Without the skip connection and for $\sigma$ a monomial of large degree, almost all fibres are 'large' (positive-dimensional in the algebraic geometry sense). For residual networks, we prove the fibres are 'small': for almost every polynomial of degree at least 5, the fibre is a singleton.

This is our headline result — it's the meaning of the title *Polynomial Residual Networks Are Generically Identifiable* — and it could help explain why residual networks are easier to train.

It was a pleasure and an honour to work on this paper with my collaborators. Most results were derived pre GPT-5.5 -- ages ago.
