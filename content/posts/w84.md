I often hear people say AI models lack taste. Much AI writing is bland, indeed. There are too many words, too few surprises. The writing is of course flawless, but it's almost too polished[^e]. Similarly, AI jokes rarely work -- they're quite repetitive, and many have a dad-joke quality. It's also unclear whether they have research taste. For example, a [paper from November 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08548) found that LLMs largely failed in matching humans judgements of interestingness.

[^e]: A sharp observation from [Sasha Gusev](https://x.com/SashaGusevPosts/status/2101178415344648563): ‘interesting reversal happening where typos are now a sign of effort‘.

Naturally, Silicon Valley is trying to teach models good taste. In a [META paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28603v1) this week, the authors trained models to optimise for 'interestingness', defined as proof length over description length in Lean. Back in February this year, the YC founder Paul Graham tweeted he thought [taste would become more important](https://x.com/paulg/status/2022604692178522562). Greg Brockman, president of openai, then called it ['a new core skill'](https://x.com/gdb/status/2023481258639286401).

Taste today is usually defined as the [ability to discern quality](https://theamericanscholar.org/matters-of-taste/), although, interestingly, until the 1700s, taste was just a physical sensation. During the Enlightenment, the word 'taste' acquired a new meaning, with Voltaire defining taste as 'capacity for discriminating' in the *Encyclopédie*.

The obsession with taste is hardly new. With the abundance of the industrial revolution came [the need to discern](https://asteriskmag.substack.com/p/what-silicon-valley-gets-wrong-about). In 1835, a parliamentary committee was formed in Britain to investigate how to improve British taste (i.e., make it as good as French taste). Some fifty years later, the Arts and Crafts movement took off, largely as a reaction to industrial mass production. The Singer machine was the slop of the 1800s.

I agree with Paul, Greg and the committee that taste is essential for businesses. Now that everyone can produce shiny b2b software, perhaps you're pitching your taste to investors, rather than your product. Still, it’s worth asking whether taste can be learned by AI.

There is one obvious argument for ‘no’. You might argue that taste requires perception, and therefore is distinctly human. But how much perception is needed for taste? And what do you even mean by perception? Reductio ad absurdum by 'you need to do philosophy of mind'. Likewise, any argument saying taste requires an appreciation of human culture would fall ('you need to do philosophy of mind').

If it's all just about discernment, I don't see why AIs couldn't learn taste. Cultivating taste within, say, painting largely comes down to studying many paintings, and AIs can 'study' more paintings within a second than a human in a lifetime[^me]. At the very least, they can make intelligent-sounding comments about many paintings.

[^me]: When I ask AI to generate 'interesting questions' based on a paper I've read, roughly 60% of questions are to my liking. Not too impressive, but it might also just be that I’m miscalibrated (when I conduct the same experiment with humans, I again get 60%).

There’s actually some evidence suggesting AIs could learn taste. The paper from November 2025 also noted that AI could generate 'engaging problems' after a screening process -- and remember that November 2025 was a long time ago[^re]. Moreover, the META paper also had moderate success: optimising for interestingness led to more novel results (queerly described as 'out-of-distribution math').

[^re]: It was before the first Erdős problems got solved. A long time ago...

There is, however, one possible way out for the believers in human-only taste. As highlighted in this [IoT episode on taste](https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b0082dzm), taste is closely related to the notion of exclusivity. If taste could be learned by AI, then we'd be flooded with... slop with style? Perhaps there's no such thing -- I don't know. But if the cheap must be tasteless, then normal LLMs have no hope of learning taste.

Regardless of whether taste can be learned by AI, you can't automate the expensive. Whatever requires human attention, then, might become a luxury good.
