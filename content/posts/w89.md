I enjoy hanging out with entrepreneurs. We rarely talk startups, though; our conversations span a wide range of topics, from technological unemployment to gossip. Part of why we have such good conversations is their way of talking. Listen carefully, and you'll see what I mean — they don't speak plain English, but founderspeak.

### Speed
Fast, fast, founderspeak! You need to convey as much information as possible within a given conversation. The [average speaking rate](https://www.quora.com/Speeches-For-the-average-person-speaking-at-a-normal-pace-what-is-the-typical-number-of-words-they-can-say-in-one-minute) is 120–150 wpm; on Dwarkesh (crash course in founderspeak), transcripts indicate it's around 180–220[^argh]. After spending more time with entrepreneurs, I've also noticed I've started speaking faster.

[^argh]: For a discussion of Dwarkesh's talking speed, see [this Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42542344).

### Ambiguity
Founderspeak is vague. Entrepreneurs love jargon and acronyms. It's partly because of the need for speed: one word is 1 second; a paraphrasing is 20. But the use of some jargon also makes you sound impressive, and it adds a bit of variation to your speech.

For entrepreneurs, there's one additional benefit to using vague language. If the listener knows your lingo, then they'll instantly feel more aligned with you and your vision (and thus be easier to convince). I remember when I figured out what B2B actually meant over the course of a fireside chat; I relished at hearing the word coming up in the conversation. I felt like part of the in-group.

### Energy
Naturally, founderspeak is full of energy. There's a lot of yes, nodding, gesturing. You cannot pitch without enthusiasm, and entrepreneurs seem unable to return to normal mode outside of the office.

### Opinions
To speak founderspeak, you should express strong opinions. Entrepreneurs love sharing hot takes and punchy slogans. It's either Option A or Option B -- there's no middleground.

I suspect this is largely because of Peter Thiel (as with so many other phenomena in the Valley). In *Zero to One*, the Builder Bible, he famously asks: 'What important truth do very few people agree with you on?' He's effectively asking about your startup ideas. Founders need to be right when others think they're wrong, so underlying each startup is one answer to his question. Now, since people know their Thiel, they're constantly on the lookout for controversial views.

### Conclusion
Sometimes I get fed up with founderspeak. Why can't they speak more like mathematicians, i.e. slowly and precisely? Why can't they admit confusion?

But founderspeak isn't about truth-seeking; it's about storytelling and, ultimately, about entertaining. I don't mean to be derogatory here -- I like this. It's like their speech adds a bit of magic to the world.
